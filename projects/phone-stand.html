Product Design · 2024

Ergonomic Mobile
Phone Stand

SolidWorks CAD FDM / PLA Mechanism Design Ergonomics Completed

A desk stand engineered around personal preference — height, tilt range, and cable clearance were all self-specified, then delivered through printable mechanisms: a ratchet-balanced parallel linkage, a lead-screw tilt actuator, and a cradle shaped for both portrait and landscape without any moving parts.

Overview

All requirements were self-specified — tilt and height ranges were chosen to cover the full gamut of personal working postures, cable bend radius at height was considered to avoid stressing the charging lead, and the cradle geometry was sized around a real device. Every mechanism had to be FDM-printable in PLA with no external hardware including 3d printed fasteners.

Height Adjustable

Scissor-style parallel linkage keeps the platform level at any height. A ratchet mechanism locks the position under load - compliant spring in the ratchet mechanism.

Tilt Adjustable

A captive-nut lead screw gives continuous, fine-grained tilt control. Range and neutral angle were set to comfortable personal viewing positions.

Portrait & Landscape

The cradle is shaped to seat the phone stably in either orientation — no rotating mechanism or swappable parts. A bottom-centre channel clears the charging lead in portrait mode.

Design Process

Three physical prototypes were made, each validating a mechanism's function, while refining the tolerance as observed per iteration.

  1. 01

    Self-Specified Requirements

    Requirements were defined from personal use: tilt and height ranges were chosen to cover comfortable viewing angles across sitting and standing postures; cable bend radius at maximum height was factored in to avoid stressing the connector; cradle width was sized to a real device. Constraint: all mechanisms printable in PLA with no external hardware.

  2. 02

    Mechanism Selection

    Evaluated telescoping columns, four-bar linkages, and scissor linkages for height. Chose a parallel linkage for its level-keeping property. Selected a ratchet latch for reliable load-bearing. Adopted a captive-nut lead screw for continuous, fine tilt control.

  3. 03

    CAD Modelling — SolidWorks

    Built a fully parametric SolidWorks assembly with mates constraining the linkage geometry and a motion study verifying tilt range. FDM tolerances (0.3 mm clearance on pivot pins, 0.2 mm on the ratchet teeth) were applied globally via design table. Cable channel geometry was validated against the measured lead dimensions of a USB-C cable.

  4. 04

    Prototype I — Proof of Concept

    First print validated the linkage kinematics and ratchet engagement. Issues found: ratchet pawl too stiff (wall too thick), pivot pins required a mallet to seat — clearances tightened too aggressively at 0.15 mm. Design updated with 0.3 mm clearances and a thinner pawl arm.

  5. 05

    Prototype II — Mechanism Refinement

    Ratchet and pivots now assembled by hand. New issue: screw actuator for tilt had thread slippage under load — the captive nut was pulling out of its pocket. Added a secondary retention tab around the nut pocket. Also widened the cable channel after testing with a braided USB-C lead that was thicker than anticipated.

  6. 06

    Prototype III — Final Build

    All mechanisms operate smoothly and reliably. The stand assembles without tools, adjusts single-handed in both axes, and accepts the phone in portrait and landscape with a stable, rattle-free hold. The cable channel clears a USB-C lead in portrait mode without stressing the connector.

Mechanisms

Ratchet Height Lock

The ratchet teeth are cut into a curved surface that follows the arc of the linkage's rotation — so each tooth engages squarely regardless of height position. A compliant lever (a thin printed arm that flexes like a spring) acts as the pawl, clicking into successive teeth as the linkage rises. No metal spring is needed; the lever's stiffness is entirely a function of wall thickness and arm length.

Getting the lever right took the most iteration of any part. Too thick and it would not flex enough to ride over teeth during adjustment; too thin and it would not hold position under the phone's weight. Tooth size was iterated in parallel — coarser teeth gave more secure locking but coarser height increments; the final geometry balances both.

  • Teeth on curved arc surface — consistent engagement at all heights
  • Compliant lever pawl — wall thickness iterated through rapid printing cycles
  • Tooth size tuned to balance height resolution vs. locking force
  • No external spring — all elasticity from printed geometry

Screw-Type Tilt Actuator

The tilt screw is entirely custom — thread profile, pitch, and nut pocket were all designed from scratch. The thread uses 45° teeth, and the nut's mating slot is cut at the same angle, giving a symmetric load-sharing contact that a square thread would not provide on a printed surface.

The screw's axis runs parallel to the tilt axis. Rotating the screw advances a link perpendicular to that axis; the link pushes the cradle arm, converting linear travel into rotational tilt. This perpendicular-motion arrangement keeps the actuator compact and clear of the phone's footprint.

Because the thread is 3D-printed, reliability is an acknowledged limitation — surface roughness and layer lines introduce variability in engagement. The mechanism works consistently in practice but is not as robust as a machined thread, and remains an area for future refinement.

  • Custom thread — 45° tooth profile for symmetric printed-surface contact
  • Screw axis parallel to tilt axis; link motion is perpendicular
  • Rotation → linear push → cradle rotation (no rack needed)
  • Reliability limited by FDM surface finish — functional, not production-grade
